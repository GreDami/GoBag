Family emergency plan

The questions to answer together, written down on one page: how you will hear, where you will meet, whom you will call and how you will leave.

Short answer

A family emergency plan answers five questions that Ready.gov lists: how you will get alerts, where you will shelter, how you will evacuate, how you will stay in touch, and whether your kit is ready. Choose two meeting places (one outside your home, one outside your neighborhood), an out-of-area contact everyone calls, who collects the children and pets, and two evacuation routes. Write it down, give everyone a copy and practice it twice a year.

Supplies are only half of being prepared. The other half is knowing, without having to discuss it in the moment, what everyone in the family will do. Emergencies rarely start when you are all at home together: one person is at work, the children are at school, a phone is flat. A plan agreed in advance is what brings everyone back together. The American Red Cross puts it in three steps: talk about the emergencies most likely where you live, give each person a responsibility, and practice.

The five questions

Ready.gov suggests starting with these, around the kitchen table:

  1. How will we receive emergency alerts and warnings? Turn on emergency alerts on every phone, sign up for your county's alerts, and know which radio station to listen to.
  2. What is our shelter plan? Where in the home you go for each risk: an interior room for a tornado, upstairs for a flood, a sealed room for smoke.
  3. What is our evacuation route? Two ways out of the neighborhood, by car and on foot, and where you would go: a relative's home, a hotel or a shelter.
  4. What is our communication plan? Who calls whom, and what you do if phones do not work.
  5. Do we need to update our kit? One go bag per person, and supplies at home.

Fill in your plan

  • Meeting place right outside the home, for a sudden emergency such as a fire
  • Meeting place outside the neighborhood, in case you cannot get home
  • Out-of-area contact: name and phone number
  • Who picks up each child from school or daycare, and a backup
  • Who checks on older relatives or neighbors who need help
  • Who takes each pet, and where pets can stay
  • Evacuation route 1 and route 2, marked on a paper map
  • Where the go bags, the first aid kit and the documents are kept
  • Where and how to turn off the water, gas and electricity, if officials tell you to
  • Medical needs: medicines, doses, allergies, devices, doctors
  • Schools', workplaces' and daycares' emergency plans and numbers
  • Insurance policy numbers and the insurer's phone number

Why an out-of-area contact

After a disaster, local phone lines are often overloaded while long-distance calls and texts still get through. Choose one relative or friend who lives far away, and have every family member call or text that person to say where they are. The Red Cross advises that everyone carry the emergency numbers in writing as well as on their phone, and that schools and daycares have them too. Texts usually get through when calls do not.

Plan for everyone in the household

Ready.gov asks you to tailor the plan to your household: the ages of its members, medical needs and equipment, disabilities, dietary needs, languages spoken, pets and service animals, and family members who are often away, such as students or people who travel for work. Each of these changes who does what, and what goes in the bags. There are guides to packing for children and for pets.

Practice it

  • Twice a year, grab your kits and drive or walk the evacuation route, as the Red Cross suggests. Try the second route too.
  • Walk to both meeting places with the children, so they know the way.
  • Test the contact chain: everyone texts the out-of-area contact on the same evening.
  • Pick the same days as your six-month kit check, so one date covers both.

Keep a copy of the plan in every go bag, on the fridge and on every phone. Update it when someone changes school or job, moves or gets a new phone number.

Frequently asked questions

What should a family emergency plan include?

How you will get alerts, where you will shelter, your evacuation routes, two meeting places, an out-of-area contact, who collects the children and pets, everyone's medical needs, and where your kits and documents are kept.

Why do I need an out-of-area emergency contact?

After a disaster, local lines are often overloaded while long-distance calls and texts still work. One contact far away gives every family member a single number to check in with.

How often should we practice our emergency plan?

Twice a year: drive or walk your evacuation routes, go to the meeting places and test the contact chain. Update the plan whenever someone changes school, job, home or phone number.

Where should we keep our emergency plan?

On paper in every go bag and on the fridge, and saved on every phone. Schools and daycares should have your emergency numbers too.
