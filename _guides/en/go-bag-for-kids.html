Go bag for kids

What to pack for babies, toddlers and school-age children, what a child can carry, and how to prepare them without scaring them.

Short answer

An adult carries the supplies: for a baby, formula, bottles, diapers, wipes, spare clothes and any medicine; for older children, their medicine, food, water and clothes. Each child old enough to carry a small backpack gets their own with a water bottle, a snack, a flashlight, a comfort toy, a rain poncho and a card with your names, phone numbers and meeting place. Practice your plan with them so they know what to do.

Children cope with emergencies better than adults expect, as long as two things are true: someone they trust has a plan, and something familiar comes with them. A child's go bag does both. It holds what the child needs that an adult kit does not, and it gives them a job and a bag of their own, which turns a frightening moment into something they are part of.

For babies and toddlers

A baby's supplies go in an adult's bag. Pack for at least three days, as for the rest of the go bag.

  • Formula and bottles, or breastfeeding supplies; ready-to-feed formula needs no clean water
  • Baby food in pouches, and a spoon
  • Diapers: count about three days' worth and add a few
  • Wipes, diaper cream and a changing mat
  • Two or three changes of clothes, a hat and a warm layer
  • A baby carrier or sling: a stroller is hard to use on rough ground
  • Any medicine, a thermometer and baby pain reliever your doctor recommends
  • A pacifier, a favorite blanket or a small soft toy
  • A copy of the birth certificate and vaccination record

A child's own backpack

From about age four or five, children can carry a small, light backpack. Keep it to things that comfort and help them; the heavy water and food stay with the adults.

  • A refillable water bottle
  • A couple of favorite snacks
  • A small flashlight or headlamp they know how to use
  • A whistle, and they know three blasts means "help"
  • A rain poncho and a warm layer
  • A change of underwear and socks
  • A comfort toy, a book, a small game, crayons and paper
  • An ID card: name, parents' names and phone numbers, an out-of-area contact, your meeting place, allergies and medicines
  • A recent photo of the child with the family, in the adult's bag too

The card that matters most

If you are separated, the card in your child's bag is how a stranger helps them back to you. Write it on paper, since phones die and young children do not remember numbers, and laminate it or put it in a zip bag. The American Red Cross advises that everyone carry emergency contacts in writing, and that schools and daycares have the same numbers. Teach older children your phone number and the out-of-area contact by heart.

When children are at school

  • Ask the school or daycare about its emergency plan: where children shelter, where they are taken if they evacuate, and how parents are told.
  • Keep your contact details up to date there, including someone who can collect your child if you cannot.
  • Agree at home who picks up which child, and tell the children: knowing who is coming is a comfort in itself.

How to prepare them without scaring them

  • Keep it practical. "If the fire alarm goes, we meet at the big tree" is reassuring; a list of disasters is not.
  • Let them pack their bag. Choosing the toy and the snacks makes it theirs.
  • Practice. Walk the evacuation route and go to the meeting place once or twice a year, as part of the family emergency plan.
  • Use their resources. The Red Cross and Ready.gov both have games, videos and activity books made for children.
  • After an emergency, expect them to be clingy or upset for a while. Keep routines going, answer their questions honestly and simply, and let them talk.

Update it as they grow

Children change faster than any kit. At every six-month check, swap clothes and diapers for the next size, replace snacks they have outgrown, update the photo and the ID card, and move up a backpack size when they can carry more.

Frequently asked questions

What should be in a child's go bag?

A water bottle, snacks, a small flashlight, a whistle, a rain poncho, a change of underwear and socks, a comfort toy or book, and a card with parents' names, phone numbers, a meeting place and any allergies or medicines.

At what age can a child carry their own go bag?

From about four or five, a small backpack with a few light things. The water, food and medicine stay in the adults' bags, and a baby's supplies always go in an adult's bag.

What do I pack for a baby in an emergency?

Formula and bottles or breastfeeding supplies, baby food, diapers, wipes and diaper cream, several changes of clothes, a carrier or sling, any medicine and a thermometer, a comfort item, and copies of the birth certificate and vaccination record.

How do I talk to my child about emergencies?

Keep it practical and calm: explain what the family will do, where you will meet and who will pick them up. Let them pack their own bag and practice the plan together, so it feels familiar rather than frightening.
