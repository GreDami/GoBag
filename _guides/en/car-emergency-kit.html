Car emergency kit

What to keep in the car for a breakdown, a snowstorm or a road that closes behind you: a checklist for the trunk, and what to add in winter.

Short answer

Keep jumper cables, a reflective triangle or flares, a flashlight, a phone charger and power bank, a first aid kit, water and snacks, a warm blanket, a paper map and basic tools in the car. In winter add an ice scraper, a small shovel, cat litter or sand for traction, gloves and a hat. Keep the tank at least half full, and check the kit every six months: a car's heat and cold age it fast.

You spend hours a week in the car, and emergencies do not wait until you get home. A breakdown on a quiet road, a snowstorm that strands traffic for a night, a flood that closes the way back: a small kit in the trunk turns each of these from frightening into uncomfortable. Ready.gov recommends keeping an emergency supply kit in the car in case you are stranded, with a few extras the car needs.

The car kit checklist

For the car

  • Jumper cables, or a portable jump starter
  • A reflective warning triangle or flares
  • A high-visibility vest
  • A spare tire, jack and wrench, checked for air
  • A tire pressure gauge, and a can of tire sealant
  • Duct tape, zip ties and a multi-tool
  • A tow strap

For you

  • Water: a few liters, more in summer
  • Snacks that keep in heat and cold: energy bars, nuts, crackers
  • A first aid kit, and a few days of any daily medicine
  • A flashlight or headlamp, with spare batteries
  • A car phone charger and a charged power bank
  • A warm blanket or emergency blanket
  • A rain poncho, gloves and comfortable walking shoes
  • A paper road map: phone signal and batteries both run out
  • A little cash
  • A whistle, and a pen and paper
  • Wet wipes, tissues and a few garbage bags

Add for winter

  • An ice scraper and snow brush
  • A small folding shovel
  • Cat litter or sand, for traction under the wheels, as Ready.gov suggests
  • Extra blankets, a hat, gloves and warm socks for everyone who rides
  • Hand warmers
  • Windshield washer fluid rated for freezing temperatures

Ready.gov also suggests winter tires with enough tread, or chains where your area requires them, and keeping the tank full in winter: it keeps the fuel line from freezing.

Add for summer and long trips

  • Extra water, more than you think: a stuck car in the sun gets dangerously hot
  • Sunscreen and a sun hat
  • A reflective sunshade
  • Coolant, and the right oil for your engine

If you are stranded

  • Pull over as far off the road as you safely can, turn on the hazard lights and set the parking brake.
  • Make yourself seen: put out the triangle or flares behind the car, and wear the vest if you get out.
  • Usually, stay with the car. It is shelter, and it is easier for rescuers to find than a person on foot.
  • If a power line falls on the car, stay inside until a trained person removes it, as Ready.gov advises.
  • In snow, keep the exhaust pipe clear before running the engine for heat, and crack a window: a blocked exhaust fills the car with carbon monoxide.
  • Never drive into floodwater. Ready.gov notes that six inches of water can make a car lose control or stall, and a foot of water floats many cars.

Before the season: have the car checked

Ready.gov lists what a mechanic should look at before an emergency: antifreeze, the battery and ignition, brakes, exhaust, fuel and air filters, the heater and defroster, lights and hazard lights, oil, the thermostat, and the windshield wipers and washer fluid. A good moment is when you swap the car kit between summer and winter.

Frequently asked questions

What should I keep in my car for emergencies?

Jumper cables, a warning triangle or flares, a flashlight, a phone charger and power bank, a first aid kit, water, snacks, a blanket, a paper map, a little cash and basic tools. Add a shovel, ice scraper and cat litter or sand in winter.

Is it OK to leave water in a hot car?

Sealed bottled water can stay in the car, but heat shortens its life and that of snacks, batteries and medicine. Replace the water and food more often than in a home kit, and check the car kit every six months.

Should I stay in my car if I am stranded in snow?

Usually, yes: the car is shelter and easier for rescuers to find. Run the engine for heat only with the exhaust pipe clear of snow and a window cracked, to avoid carbon monoxide.

Why keep cat litter in the car?

Spread under the drive wheels, cat litter or sand gives tires grip on ice and packed snow. Ready.gov includes it in its car kit.
