First aid kit checklist

What a home first aid kit should hold, item by item and in what quantity, what to add for your family, and how to keep it from quietly expiring.

Short answer

The American Red Cross's list for a family of four includes 25 adhesive bandages in assorted sizes, sterile gauze pads, compress dressings, a roller bandage, two triangular bandages, adhesive tape, antiseptic wipes, antibiotic and hydrocortisone ointment, an instant cold compress, non-latex gloves, tweezers, a non-glass thermometer, a breathing barrier, an emergency blanket and first aid instructions. Add your family's medicines and emergency numbers, keep one kit at home and one in the car, and check the dates regularly.

A first aid kit is the part of an emergency kit you are most likely to use: a cut in the kitchen, a sprain on a walk, a burn from the stove. That is also why it empties without anyone noticing. The Red Cross recommends keeping a kit at home and one in the car, knowing where the kit is at work, and checking yours regularly so used and out-of-date items get replaced.

The Red Cross list for a family of four

  • 2 absorbent compress dressings (5 × 9 inches)
  • 25 adhesive bandages in assorted sizes
  • 1 roll of adhesive cloth tape (10 yards × 1 inch)
  • 5 antibiotic ointment packets (about 1 g each)
  • 5 antiseptic wipe packets
  • 2 packets of aspirin (81 mg each)
  • 1 emergency blanket
  • 1 breathing barrier, with a one-way valve
  • 1 instant cold compress
  • 2 pairs of non-latex gloves, size large
  • 2 hydrocortisone ointment packets (about 1 g each)
  • 1 roller gauze bandage, 3 inches wide
  • 1 roller bandage, 4 inches wide
  • 5 sterile gauze pads, 3 × 3 inches
  • 5 sterile gauze pads, 4 × 4 inches
  • An oral thermometer, non-mercury and non-glass
  • 2 triangular bandages
  • Tweezers
  • A first aid instruction booklet

Aspirin is on the list for adults; it is not for children or teenagers, and not for everyone, so check with your doctor.

Add for your household

  • Each person's prescription medicine, with a written list of doses
  • Pain and fever relievers for adults, and children's versions in the right strength
  • Antihistamines for allergic reactions; an epinephrine auto-injector if a doctor has prescribed one
  • Anti-diarrhea medicine and oral rehydration salts
  • Blister plasters and an elastic bandage for sprains
  • Small scissors and safety pins
  • Saline solution to rinse eyes and wounds
  • Burn gel or dressings
  • Emergency phone numbers: your doctor, poison control, an out-of-area contact

The smaller kit for your go bag

A go bag needs a lighter version: a dozen bandages, a few gauze pads and wipes, tape, gloves, tweezers, an emergency blanket, pain relievers, at least a week of each person's prescription medicine and spare glasses. The rest of the go bag checklist covers what goes around it, and the car emergency kit needs a kit of its own.

Where to keep it

Somewhere cool, dry and easy to reach for adults, out of reach of small children: a high kitchen cupboard or a hall closet, not the bathroom, where steam damages dressings and medicine. Tell everyone in the house where it is. A clear or labeled box makes it obvious what is missing.

Check it every six months

  • Replace anything used since the last check
  • Check the dates on medicine, ointments and wipes
  • Make sure packets are sealed and gloves have not perished
  • Replace the thermometer's battery
  • Update the medicine list and the phone numbers

A kit is only as good as the person using it. A first aid course takes a few hours and teaches what no booklet can; the Red Cross runs them in person and online.

Frequently asked questions

What are the essential items in a first aid kit?

Adhesive bandages, sterile gauze pads and dressings, a roller bandage, adhesive tape, antiseptic wipes, antibiotic ointment, non-latex gloves, tweezers, a cold compress, a thermometer, an emergency blanket and instructions, plus your family's own medicines.

How often should I check my first aid kit?

Every six months, and after each time you use it. Replace what was used, check the dates on medicine and ointments, and update your medicine list and emergency numbers.

Is a store-bought first aid kit good enough?

It is a good base, and the Red Cross sells them itself. Check it against the list above and add what it lacks, especially your family's prescription medicine and children's versions of pain relievers.

Where should I keep a first aid kit at home?

In a cool, dry place that adults can reach quickly and small children cannot, such as a high kitchen cupboard. Avoid the bathroom, where humidity spoils dressings and medicine, and keep a second kit in the car.
