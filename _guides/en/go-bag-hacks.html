Go bag hacks

Twenty small tricks that make an emergency bag lighter, drier, cheaper and quicker to grab when you have minutes to leave.

Short answer

The tricks that matter most: line the bag with a heavy garbage bag and pack by category in zip bags, keep a headlamp in the top pocket, tape a "last-minute grab list" inside the lid for things that cannot live in the bag (phone, glasses, fridge medicine, pets), split cash between two places, download offline maps, and check everything on the day the clocks change. Build it from what you already own and add one item per shopping trip.

A go bag checklist tells you what to pack. These are the small things that decide whether the bag works on the day: whether you find the flashlight in the dark, whether the clothes are dry after a walk in the rain, and whether the bag gets packed at all.

Packing

  1. Line the bag with a heavy garbage bag. Few backpacks are waterproof. A liner keeps everything dry, and the bag itself becomes a poncho, a ground sheet or a way to carry water.
  2. Pack by category in zip bags. One for first aid, one for documents, one for hygiene, one for chargers. You find things by feel, and a leak stays in one bag.
  3. Headlamp on top. Light is the first thing you need when the power is out. Keep a headlamp in the lid or top pocket, not at the bottom: it leaves both hands free.
  4. Heavy things close to your back. Water and food in the middle of the bag against your spine; light, bulky things at the bottom and outside. The same weight feels much lighter.
  5. Roll clothes and squeeze the air out. A spare outfit rolled tight in a zip bag takes far less space and stays dry.
  6. Wrap duct tape around a pen or a water bottle. You carry a few meters of it with no roll to pack.

Grabbing it in a hurry

  1. Tape a "last-minute grab list" inside the lid. Some things cannot live in the bag: your phone and charger, wallet and keys, glasses, medicine from the fridge, the laptop, the pets. In a hurry nobody remembers them; a list does.
  2. Keep the bag where you leave. By the door or in the closet on the way out, never in the attic or behind the boxes in the garage.
  3. One color per person. Colored pouches or tags tell everyone which bag and which first aid kit is whose, even in the dark.
  4. Walk around the block with it. Ten minutes with the bag on tells you if it is too heavy or the straps need adjusting, before it matters.

Money, phone and papers

  1. Split the cash. Small bills in two places, such as the bag and a pocket, so you are not left with nothing if one goes missing.
  2. Write the important numbers on paper. A card in the bag and in your wallet: family, the out-of-area contact, your doctor. Phones run out of battery.
  3. Download offline maps of your area and your evacuation routes, and keep a paper map as a backup.
  4. Tape the cable to the power bank. A charged power bank with no cable is just a weight.
  5. Give an old phone a second life. Charged and switched off, with offline maps on it, it is a spare flashlight, map and camera.

Water and food

  1. Pack food you like. Emergency ration bars keep for years, but a familiar snack makes a bad day better, especially for children. Rotate it into lunchboxes before it expires.
  2. Freeze a few bottles of water. In an outage they keep the freezer cold longer, and once they thaw you have drinking water.

Saving money

  1. Start with what you own. An old backpack, a spare charger, travel-size toiletries from hotels, an old pair of glasses: half a go bag is already in your home.
  2. Add one item per shopping trip. A pack of batteries this week, a first aid kit next. Spread over a month or two, the cost disappears into ordinary shopping.
  3. Check it when the clocks change. Twice a year is easy to remember, and it catches expired food, flat batteries and outgrown clothes before they matter. The maintenance guide has the routine.

Bonus: small comforts

Earplugs and an eye mask for a noisy shelter, a deck of cards, a phone charging cable long enough to reach from the floor, a pair of broken-in socks. None of them is on an official list, and each makes a night away from home a little easier.

Frequently asked questions

How do I keep my go bag waterproof?

Line the backpack with a heavy garbage bag or a dry bag, and pack items by category in zip bags. Even if the outside gets soaked, the clothes, documents and electronics stay dry.

How can I make a go bag lighter?

Put the heaviest things against your back, carry a filter instead of most of the water, roll clothes tightly, and take out duplicates. If you cannot walk with it for an hour, it is still too heavy.

How do I build a go bag on a budget?

Start with what you already own, such as an old backpack, spare chargers and travel toiletries, then add one item per shopping trip. Store food you already eat and rotate it, so nothing is wasted.

What do people forget to pack in a go bag?

A paper list of phone numbers, spare glasses, a phone cable, cash in small bills, a change of socks, and the things that cannot live in the bag, such as fridge medicine. A grab list taped inside the lid covers those.
